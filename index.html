Glaucoma medical therapy
Mechanism — trabecular versus uveoscleral outflow:
- Miotics (e.g., pilocarpine) contract the ciliary muscle, which pulls on the scleral spur to widen trabecular meshwork spaces and increase trabecular (conventional) outflow — but the very same ciliary muscle contraction narrows the uveoscleral outflow pathway, closing the spaces aqueous would otherwise drain through. Miotics are therefore the outlier among major drug classes: they increase one outflow route while decreasing the other.
- Cycloplegics, adrenergic agents, and prostaglandin analogues all increase uveoscleral outflow instead — cycloplegics by relaxing the ciliary muscle (widening the spaces between muscle bundles), and prostaglandins being the most potent and clinically important example of this mechanism.
- Practical consequence: combining a miotic with a prostaglandin (or another uveoscleral-outflow-enhancing agent) does not produce simple additive IOP lowering, since the two drug classes act on the same anatomic structure (the ciliary muscle and adjacent outflow pathways) in opposing directions — part of why this combination is uncommon in modern practice.


Pediatric population — the brimonidine contraindication:
- Brimonidine (an alpha-2 agonist) is a lipophilic molecule that readily crosses the immature blood-brain barrier, producing potentially severe CNS depression, apnea, bradycardia, and hypotension even at standard topical ophthalmic doses.
- This makes brimonidine specifically contraindicated in children younger than 2 years old — the most consistently repeated pediatric pharmacology fact in this exam bank's glaucoma section, given documented case reports of serious, sometimes life-threatening, CNS and cardiorespiratory depression in this age group.
- In children 2 years of age and older, this risk diminishes sufficiently that alpha-2 agonists can be considered (with appropriate caution) as a treatment option — making the cutoff itself, not a blanket ban on all pediatric use, the tested detail.
- When treating glaucoma in infants and toddlers, clinicians generally favor topical carbonic anhydrase inhibitors and beta-blockers (with appropriate dosing and punctal occlusion to limit systemic absorption) as safer first-line medical options — recognizing, though, that medical therapy alone is rarely sufficient long-term in primary congenital glaucoma, where surgery remains definitive.


Pregnancy and breastfeeding:
- Prostaglandin analogues (e.g., latanoprost/Xalatan) carry a recognized uterotonic effect — a real, clinically significant risk of stimulating uterine contractions and precipitating miscarriage or preterm labor. Describing prostaglandin use in pregnancy as "unlikely to affect the fetus" understates this established risk and is a tested false statement; prostaglandins are generally avoided first-line in pregnant patients.
- Systemic (oral) carbonic anhydrase inhibitors have demonstrated teratogenic effects in animal studies and are avoided during pregnancy out of caution; they are also generally avoided during breastfeeding given limited human safety data.
- Alpha-2 agonists (brimonidine) are generally used with caution in pregnancy (sometimes considered acceptable earlier in pregnancy) but should be discontinued before delivery to avoid the same neonatal CNS-depression risk described above, and are generally considered unsuitable during breastfeeding given direct infant exposure via milk.
- Topical beta-blockers are, by contrast, among the more commonly used glaucoma medications in both pregnancy and breastfeeding when treatment is necessary — used cautiously with attention to minimizing systemic absorption (e.g., punctal occlusion) and monitoring the infant for bradycardia or hypoglycemia. Characterizing beta-blockers as unsuitable for use in both populations is a tested false statement, though recommendations on this point have evolved over time and vary somewhat by source.
- General practice when glaucoma medical therapy is unavoidable in pregnancy/breastfeeding: lowest effective concentration and frequency of the best-tolerated agent, punctal occlusion or lid closure after instillation, and close collaboration with the patient's obstetrician/pediatrician.


Systemic comorbidities and disease-specific cautions:
- Beta-blockers are avoided in COPD/asthma (bronchospasm risk via systemic absorption).
- Oral carbonic anhydrase inhibitors are avoided/cautioned against in patients with significant hepatic impairment (e.g., cirrhosis), due to risk of precipitating or worsening hepatic encephalopathy through altered acid-base and ammonia handling.
- Brimonidine has no specific recognized contraindication in carotid-cavernous sinus fistula-related glaucoma (a condition driven by elevated episcleral venous pressure rather than any mechanism brimonidine would worsen).
- Pilocarpine/miotics are relatively contraindicated in uveitic glaucoma — miosis and ciliary muscle contraction can worsen inflammation, increase ciliary-spasm pain, and promote posterior synechiae against an inflamed iris/lens; moreover, pilocarpine's trabecular-outflow-enhancing mechanism is far less effective when the trabecular meshwork itself is already obstructed by inflammatory cells, fibrin, or PAS — the actual driver of most uveitic glaucoma.
- By contrast, beta-blockers remain an acceptable option in neovascular glaucoma as part of maximal medical therapy, and pilocarpine remains an accepted (if older-generation) option in ordinary POAG — the contraindication is specific to the inflammatory mechanism, not to miotics or beta-blockers generally.
- Topical CAIs (e.g., dorzolamide) have no specific contraindication in Sturge-Weber syndrome — that condition's glaucoma mechanism relates to elevated episcleral venous pressure from the choroidal/episcleral vascular anomaly, with the syndrome-specific risk (choroidal effusion) being a surgical, not medical-therapy, concern.


Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors — additional detail:
- Topical CAIs can precipitate corneal decompensation in eyes with marginal/compromised endothelial cell function, since endothelial carbonic anhydrase activity contributes to the corneal endothelial pump mechanism.
- Sulfonamide cross-reactivity: patients with a history of serious sulfonamide allergy should generally avoid CAIs, given their sulfonamide-derived structure.
- Ceiling effect: adding a topical CAI on top of an already-maximal-dose oral CAI provides no additional IOP-lowering benefit, since both target the same enzyme.
- Serious oral CAI (acetazolamide) systemic side effects are more classically hypokalemia, metabolic acidosis, renal stones, paresthesias, and (rarely) blood dyscrasias such as aplastic anemia or Stevens-Johnson syndrome. Hypocalcemia is not a standard, frequently cited serious oral CAI side effect — a detail this exam bank flags with some genuine source-dependent ambiguity. Periodic electrolyte monitoring is often recommended for patients on chronic oral acetazolamide, particularly those also on other potassium-depleting medications (e.g., diuretics).


Pharmacodynamic timing — onset and peak effect:
- Brimonidine, timolol, and dorzolamide all reach peak IOP-lowering effect relatively quickly, typically within roughly 2 hours of instillation.
- Prostaglandin analogues have a notably delayed peak effect, often cited as occurring approximately 8–12 hours after instillation — consistent with their prolonged duration of action and the reason they are conventionally dosed in the evening for once-daily use. This delayed-onset profile is clinically relevant when interpreting same-day IOP checks performed shortly after a patient's morning prostaglandin dose.


Newer agents — Rho kinase inhibitors:
- Rho kinase inhibitors (e.g., netarsudil) lower IOP by directly enhancing trabecular meshwork outflow (a distinct mechanism from the ciliary-muscle-mediated pathways above).
- Their most common adverse effect, by a clear margin in clinical trials, is conjunctival hyperemia (redness) — more frequent than subconjunctival hemorrhage, punctate keratitis, or corneal verticillata (the last being more classically associated with amiodarone or chloroquine-type drugs). This hyperemia is generally not sight-threatening but is clinically significant for patient tolerability and treatment adherence, warranting pre-treatment counseling.


Beta-blocker systemic absorption and punctal occlusion:
- Topical beta-blockers (e.g., timolol) effectively lower IOP by reducing aqueous humor production, have a reasonably long duration of action (often twice-daily dosing), and carry well-known systemic risks — bradycardia, bronchospasm, and masked hypoglycemia symptoms — via nasolacrimal absorption into a highly vascular surface that bypasses first-pass hepatic metabolism.
- Punctal occlusion (pressing on the nasolacrimal duct after instillation) DOES reduce systemic absorption and is a standard, effective counseling point — denying this benefit is a tested false statement. Simply closing the eyes for 1–2 minutes after instillation achieves a similar effect.


Drug- & steroid-induced glaucoma
Corticosteroid-induced glaucoma ("steroid response"):
- Risk factors: primary open-angle glaucoma, first-degree relatives of POAG patients, very young age (<6 years) AND older age (both ends of the age spectrum are higher risk, not just children — describing the elderly as lower-risk is a tested false statement), high myopia (not hyperopia — hyperopia carries no established association and is the tested distractor), diabetes mellitus, and connective tissue disease (e.g., rheumatoid arthritis).
- Can occur via any route of administration — topical, periocular/intraocular injection, inhaled, or oral — not topical use alone.
- Time course: clinically significant IOP rise typically takes longer than 5 days to manifest and is uncommon within 2 weeks in most patients.
- Classic teaching statistic: with topical dexamethasone used for 6 weeks or more, approximately 4% of patients develop IOP rising above 31 mmHg.
- Generally reversible with drug discontinuation, but close IOP monitoring remains essential throughout treatment, since prolonged unrecognized elevation can still cause permanent glaucomatous damage before reversal.
- Relative potency for raising IOP: dexamethasone has a greater IOP-raising propensity than fluorometholone (FML) — not less. This is the clinical rationale for "soft steroids" such as fluorometholone, loteprednol etabonate, and rimexolone, which are specifically formulated (loteprednol, in particular, engineered to be rapidly metabolized to an inactive form) to retain anti-inflammatory effect while minimizing IOP elevation risk — useful when treating ocular surface or mild anterior segment inflammation in glaucoma-susceptible patients.
- Because steroid-response risk varies so widely between individuals, any patient on prolonged corticosteroid therapy (regardless of route or indication) who falls into one of the higher-risk categories above warrants closer-interval IOP monitoring than a low-risk patient on the same treatment.


Topiramate-induced acute angle closure — a non-pupillary-block mimic of malignant glaucoma:
- Mechanism: ciliary body edema and anterior rotation push the lens-iris diaphragm forward, narrowing the angle — a mechanism with no pupillary-block component, directly analogous to malignant glaucoma.
- Because there is no pupillary block, laser iridotomy is NOT effective treatment and does not address the underlying mechanism — recommending urgent LI for topiramate-induced angle closure is a tested false statement.
- Miotics would worsen the condition (promoting further anterior ciliary body rotation, exactly as in malignant glaucoma), and laser trabeculoplasty is not indicated since the pathology is not trabecular meshwork dysfunction.
- Correct management: cycloplegic agents (e.g., atropine), which relax the ciliary muscle and pull the lens-iris diaphragm posteriorly, together with prompt discontinuation of the offending drug and aqueous suppressants.
- Clinical clues distinguishing it from ordinary pupillary-block angle closure: typically occurs within days to weeks of starting topiramate (commonly prescribed for migraine prophylaxis or weight loss), is often bilateral, and presents with an acute myopic shift — pupillary-block angle closure, by contrast, is far more often unilateral/asymmetric and not associated with a sudden myopic shift.
- Other drugs causing the identical ciliochoroidal-effusion/angle-closure syndrome: other sulfonamides (including acetazolamide — notable since it is itself a glaucoma drug) and bupropion have been reported to cause the same mechanism.
- Clinical course (applies across all causative drugs): presentation is usually within a month of starting the drug, with blurred vision, sometimes haloes, ocular pain, and redness. Signs include a shallow anterior chamber and raised IOP. Treatment is lowering IOP and stopping the drug; prognosis is good if recognized promptly.


Systemic drugs that can precipitate pupillary-block angle closure (a separate, more familiar mechanism):
- Many systemic drug classes with anticholinergic or sympathomimetic properties can dilate the pupil, potentially precipitating acute angle closure in eyes with anatomically predisposed narrow angles. This category includes antidepressants (particularly tricyclics), antinausea/antiemetic medications, antispasmodics, and — beyond the specific classes tested directly — antihistamines, decongestants, and antipsychotics.
- Nonselective adrenergic agonists (e.g., epinephrine compounds) are avoided in primary angle closure for the same pupil-dilation reason.
- Clinical implication: screening for narrow/occludable angles before starting these drug classes in at-risk patients, and counseling known narrow-angle patients about acute angle-closure symptoms, is good practice before initiating any of them.


Other notable glaucoma-medication and topical side effects:
- Pilocarpine (a parasympathomimetic/cholinergic agent) induces ciliary muscle contraction, increasing lens curvature and accommodative tone, producing a myopic shift — not a hyperopic shift, which is the tested reversal of this well-established direction.
- Prostaglandin analogues carry a recognized risk of cystic macular edema, particularly in eyes with a disrupted posterior capsule or active inflammation.
- Topical beta-blockers carry a recognized risk of inducing bronchospasm via systemic absorption, and are also the topical glaucoma medication class most classically associated with aqueous-deficient dry eye — through blockade of β-adrenergic receptors on the lacrimal gland (reducing tear secretion) plus reduced corneal sensation blunting the reflex tearing arc — more specifically than alpha-2 agonists, carbonic anhydrase inhibitors, or prostaglandin analogues. This is one reason preservative-free formulations or alternative first-line agents (e.g., prostaglandin analogues, which have a more favorable ocular surface tolerability profile) are often preferred in patients with significant pre-existing dry eye disease.
- Alpha-2 agonists (e.g., brimonidine) commonly cause dry mouth as a systemic side effect.


Non-drug physiologic factors affecting IOP (for contrast):
- Breath holding causes transient CO2 retention and a Valsalva-like venous pressure increase, producing a recognized transient elevation of IOP.
- By contrast, aerobic exercise, alcohol consumption, and pregnancy all lower IOP (through increased outflow facility, diuretic/vasodilatory effects, and hormonal/outflow changes respectively) — a contrast this exam bank tests repeatedly from different angles.


Interactions & contraindications
Topical ophthalmic drugs are absorbed systemically (nasolacrimal mucosa bypasses first-pass metabolism), so "just eye drops" can meaningfully interact with systemic drugs and comorbidities. Organise this two ways: (A) each topical class and what alters or forbids its use, and (B) systemic drugs that damage the eye and therefore change ophthalmic monitoring/management. See also and.


A. Topical ophthalmic classes — interactions and contraindications


Beta-blockers (timolol, betaxolol, carteolol, levobunolol). The highest-yield interaction set among eye drops.
- Respiratory disease: contraindicated / avoid in asthma and severe COPD (bronchospasm); betaxolol (β1-selective) is comparatively safer but not risk-free.
- Cardiac: contraindicated in sinus bradycardia, second/third-degree AV block, and decompensated heart failure. Additive with systemic beta-blockers, verapamil/diltiazem (non-dihydropyridine CCBs), and digoxin → bradycardia and heart block; regimen change or switch class if the patient is already on these.
- Diabetes: can mask the adrenergic warning signs of hypoglycaemia — caution and counselling in insulin-treated patients.
- Mitigate systemic load with punctal occlusion and lowest concentration; if contraindicated, switch to a prostaglandin analogue or topical CAI.


Alpha-2 agonists (brimonidine, apraclonidine).
- Contraindicated with MAO inhibitors, and caution with tricyclic antidepressants and other drugs affecting noradrenergic transmission (hypertensive/hypotensive interactions).
- Additive CNS/respiratory depression with alcohol, barbiturates, opioids, sedatives.
- Contraindicated in infants/young children (apnoea, CNS depression) —.


Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors — topical (dorzolamide, brinzolamide) and oral (acetazolamide).
- Sulfonamide allergy: these are sulfonamide derivatives — avoid in significant sulfa hypersensitivity.
- High-dose salicylates/aspirin: dangerous interaction — salicylates displace acetazolamide and both cause metabolic acidosis; combined use can precipitate severe, even fatal, acidosis and CNS toxicity. Avoid oral CAIs with high-dose aspirin.
- Sickle cell disease/trait: oral CAIs cause systemic acidosis that can precipitate sickling (relevant when lowering IOP in hyphaema) — avoid; use alternative agents.
- Topiramate (another CA inhibitor): additive acidosis, renal stones, and its own risk of drug-induced acute angle-closure/transient myopia — see below.
- Avoid oral CAIs in hepatic/renal impairment, severe hypokalaemia, and with other drugs causing hypokalaemia (thiazides, corticosteroids, digoxin toxicity risk).


Prostaglandin analogues (latanoprost, travoprost, bimatoprost).
- Relative caution in active intraocular inflammation, uveitis, and cystoid macular oedema / aphakic-pseudophakic eyes with torn posterior capsule (may worsen inflammation/CMO).
- Caution in a history of herpetic keratitis (possible reactivation).
- Generally the safest class for systemic comorbidity (asthma, cardiac disease) — often the switch target when beta-blockers are contraindicated.


Anticholinergic mydriatics/cycloplegics (atropine, cyclopentolate, homatropine, tropicamide).
- Can precipitate acute angle-closure in anatomically narrow/occludable angles — screen before dilating;.
- Additive systemic anticholinergic load with oral anticholinergics, antihistamines, TCAs → confusion (elderly), urinary retention, tachycardia.
- Heightened systemic sensitivity in infants and Down syndrome.


Sympathomimetic mydriatic (phenylephrine).
- Hypertensive crisis risk with MAO inhibitors, TCAs, and in uncontrolled hypertension/cardiovascular disease. Use 2.5% (not 10%) generally, and especially in children and cardiac patients.


Miotics (pilocarpine).
- Contraindicated/caution where miosis/ciliary spasm is harmful: rhegmatogenous retinal detachment risk, active anterior uveitis; systemic cholinergic effects (bronchospasm — caution in asthma, bradycardia, GI upset).


Topical corticosteroids (prednisolone, dexamethasone, difluprednate, loteprednol).
- Contraindicated in active epithelial herpes simplex (dendritic) keratitis and most untreated ocular infections (fungal, mycobacterial) — can cause devastating worsening.
- Steroid responders (children, high myopes, POAG/glaucoma-family patients) develop IOP rise and posterior subcapsular cataract — monitor IOP;.
- Combined topical steroid + topical NSAID markedly raises corneal melt/perforation risk, especially in dry eye, neurotrophic cornea, or post-surgical epithelial defects.


Topical NSAIDs (ketorolac, diclofenac, bromfenac, nepafenac).
- Risk of corneal melt/sterile ulceration, magnified by concurrent steroids, ocular surface disease, diabetes, and epithelial defects; caution with aspirin/NSAID hypersensitivity.


Topical antibiotics.
- Aminoglycosides — surface toxicity/punctate keratopathy with prolonged use.
- Fluoroquinolones — precipitate deposits (ciprofloxacin) in epithelial defects; theoretical additive systemic risks are minimal from topical use but relevant if the child/adult is also on systemic fluoroquinolones.
- Chloramphenicol — very rare idiosyncratic aplastic anaemia is classically cited; regional practice varies.

These do not "interact" with a drop but force a monitoring protocol, dose ceiling, or drug avoidance in ophthalmology:


    

  • Hydroxychloroquine / chloroquine → bull's-eye maculopathy; weight-based dose ceiling and mandatory screening (baseline + annual after 5 years). See.

  • Ethambutol / isoniazid / amiodarone / vigabatrin / methotrexate → toxic optic neuropathy (and amiodarone keratopathy); baseline and interval vision/colour/field checks. See.

  • Alpha-1 blockers (tamsulosin and related) → intraoperative floppy iris syndrome (IFIS) — must be flagged before cataract surgery so the surgeon alters technique (stopping the drug rarely reverses it). See.

  • Topiramate (and other sulfa-based agents) → idiosyncratic acute angle-closure with transient myopia (bilateral, ciliochoroidal effusion) — stop the drug; this angle closure is not managed with a peripheral iridotomy.
    • 

  • Corticosteroids (systemic/inhaled/periocular) → IOP rise and cataract.
    • 

  • Bisphosphonates and immune checkpoint inhibitors, rifabutin, cidofovir → drug-induced uveitis —.
    • 

  • Phenothiazines (thioridazine, chlorpromazine) and digoxin → retinopathy / xanthopsia —.
    • 

  • Fingolimod, thiazolidinediones (glitazones), taxanes, niacin, latanoprost → drug-associated macular oedema (consider in unexplained CMO).
    • 

  • Sildenafil/PDE5 inhibitors → transient blue-tinged vision, and caution/association with NAION.

  • Anticoagulants/antiplatelets → alter timing and counselling for intraocular surgery and intravitreal injection (usually continued for injections; individualised for major surgery).

Paediatric medications
Why children are not small adults (governing principles)


The single most important concept is that a topical eye drop delivers a near-adult absolute dose to a much smaller body. A standard drop is ~30–50 µL regardless of the patient's size, so an infant weighing 3–4 kg receives a systemic dose per kilogram many times that of an adult. Systemic absorption is also greater per drop in the young: thinner conjunctival/nasal mucosa, proportionally larger nasolacrimal drainage relative to ocular surface, and an immature blood–brain barrier and hepatic/renal clearance. The practical consequences that recur in exams and clinics:


    

  • Use the lowest effective concentration, and prefer weaker formulations in neonates and infants (e.g. cyclopentolate 0.5% rather than 1–2%; phenylephrine 2.5% rather than 10%).
    • 

  • Punctal (nasolacrimal) occlusion or simple eyelid closure for ~1–2 minutes after instillation markedly reduces systemic absorption and is a cheap, examinable safety measure — especially for cycloplegics and IOP-lowering drops.
    • 

  • Ointments are often preferred in infants — longer contact time (helpful given poor cooperation and reflex tearing that washes drops out) and slower systemic uptake than drops.
    • 

  • Blot excess from the lid margin/cheek after instilling.
    • 

  • Titrate oral agents by weight (mg/kg), and watch metabolic/electrolyte effects that children tolerate poorly.
    • 



Cycloplegics and mydriatics (refraction and fundus exam)


Accurate paediatric refraction requires cycloplegia because children have powerful accommodation that masks hyperopia.


    

  • Cyclopentolate is the standard cycloplegic for refraction. Typical: cyclopentolate 1%, one drop, repeated once after ~5 minutes, refract at ~30–40 minutes. In infants use 0.5%; avoid 2% in neonates. Key toxicities are CNS (drowsiness, ataxia, disorientation, rarely seizures) and GI (reduced gut motility/feeding intolerance) — counsel parents and consider withholding a feed briefly around instillation in small infants.
    • 

  • Atropine 1% is the most powerful, longest-acting cycloplegic (onset slow; effect lasts up to ~1–2 weeks). Reserved for dense accommodative tone (e.g. some cases of accommodative esotropia work-up) or amblyopia penalisation (below). Watch for anticholinergic toxicity — fever, flushing, tachycardia, dry mouth, delirium ("hot, dry, red, blind, mad"); ointment/lower concentration reduces systemic load in the very young.
    • 

  • Tropicamide 0.5–1% — short-acting mydriatic, weak cycloplegic; good for dilated fundoscopy, not adequate for refraction alone.
    • 

  • Phenylephrine for additive mydriasis: use 2.5%, never 10%, in children/neonates — the 10% strength has caused systemic hypertension and is contraindicated in infants and in cardiovascular disease.
    • 



ROP screening dilation classically uses a low-concentration combined drop — cyclopentolate 0.2% / phenylephrine 1% (Cyclomydril), or dilute tropicamide + phenylephrine — precisely to limit systemic anticholinergic/adrenergic load in preterm neonates. See.


Atropine for amblyopia (penalisation) and for myopia control


Two distinct, commonly confused uses of atropine:


    

  • Amblyopia penalisation: atropine 1% to the sound (better) eye blurs its near vision, forcing use of the amblyopic eye — an alternative to occlusion (patching). Evidence (PEDIG/ATS studies) shows weekend-only atropine (2 days/week) is as effective as daily for moderate amblyopia, and comparable to patching. See.
    • 

  • Myopia progression control: low-dose atropine (0.01%, 0.025%, or 0.05%) nightly slows axial elongation. 0.01% has the fewest side effects (minimal photophobia/near blur) but weaker effect and more rebound on stopping; 0.05% gives the best efficacy-to-tolerability balance in the ATOM/LAMP data. This is a chronic regimen distinct from the concentrated penalisation use above. See.
    • 



Paediatric glaucoma medical therapy (bridging/adjunct to surgery)


In primary congenital/infantile glaucoma surgery (goniotomy/trabeculotomy) is the definitive treatment; drops are usually a temporising or adjunctive measure. Safety dominates drug choice — and.


    

  • Brimonidine (alpha-2 agonist) is CONTRAINDICATED in infants and young children (classically <2 years, and avoided in low-weight children) — it crosses the immature blood–brain barrier and causes CNS depression, somnolence, bradycardia, hypotension, hypothermia, and life-threatening apnoea. This is one of the highest-yield paediatric ophthalmology safety facts.
    • 

  • Topical carbonic anhydrase inhibitors (dorzolamide, brinzolamide) are the generally preferred/safest first-line class in infants — minimal systemic effect topically.
    • 

  • Beta-blockers (timolol) can be used but with caution: risk of bradycardia, bronchospasm/apnoea; use the lowest concentration (e.g. 0.25%), consider once-daily, and use punctal occlusion. Avoid in reactive airway disease.
    • 

  • Prostaglandin analogues (latanoprost) are relatively safe but have variable/limited efficacy in congenital glaucoma (the trabecular/uveoscleral outflow mechanism is less developed); more useful in older children and juvenile open-angle glaucoma.
    • 

  • Oral acetazolamide (~10–15 mg/kg/day divided) is used as a temporising systemic agent; monitor for metabolic acidosis, poor feeding, paraesthesia, and electrolyte disturbance.
    • 



Neonatal and paediatric anti-infectives


Ophthalmia neonatorum (conjunctivitis in the first 4 weeks) — timing and organism drive the regimen; :


    

  • Gonococcal (N. gonorrhoeae, hyperacute, days 1–5, profuse purulent discharge, corneal-perforation risk): an emergency requiring systemic therapy — single-dose ceftriaxone (or cefotaxime if hyperbilirubinaemic) plus frequent saline lavage; topical alone is inadequate. Treat mother/partners.
    • 

  • Chlamydial (C. trachomatis, days 5–14, mucopurulent): requires oral (systemic) erythromycin (topical does not eradicate nasopharyngeal carriage / prevent pneumonitis). Counsel on the association between oral erythromycin in neonates and infantile hypertrophic pyloric stenosis (IHPS).
    • 

  • Prophylaxis (Credé): historically silver nitrate; modern practice uses erythromycin 0.5% ointment at birth (against gonococcus; note limited efficacy against chlamydia).
    • 



Paediatric bacterial conjunctivitis/keratitis: broad-spectrum topical antibiotics (e.g. chloramphenicol where used, fusidic acid, aminoglycoside, or a fluoroquinolone for keratitis). Preseptal/orbital cellulitis in children more often needs systemic antibiotics and imaging — a red flag, not a drops problem.


Ocular surface and allergy in children


    

  • Allergic conjunctivitis / vernal keratoconjunctivitis: dual-action antihistamine/mast-cell stabilisers (olopatadine, ketotifen) first-line; topical steroids only short-course under supervision for severe VKC because of the paediatric propensity for steroid-induced IOP rise and cataract; topical ciclosporin as a steroid-sparing option in severe/recalcitrant VKC. See.
    • 

  • Congenital nasolacrimal duct obstruction: usually no antibiotic unless superimposed infection; management is lacrimal sac massage (Crigler) with most resolving by ~12 months before considering probing. See.
    • 



Steroids — the paediatric caution


Children are stronger topical-steroid responders than adults: a lower threshold for steroid-induced ocular hypertension and faster posterior subcapsular cataract formation. Use the weakest effective steroid for the shortest time, monitor IOP, and prefer steroid-sparing agents (NSAIDs, ciclosporin, mast-cell stabilisers) where possible. See.

Immunomodulatory therapy (uveitis)
Steroid dosing strategy:
- Start at an adequately high dose to gain control, then taper — never
 start low and escalate.
- 7.5 mg/day prednisone-equivalent is the standard clinical threshold: if
 oral steroid cannot be tapered below this without flare, add steroid-sparing
 IMT rather than maintaining long-term higher-dose steroid.
- Oral steroid ≥20 mg/day maintained beyond 2–3 weeks should not be stopped
 abruptly, given HPA-axis suppression risk.
- A "saw-tooth" pattern of repeated relapse each time steroid is tapered is
 itself a clinical signal to slow the taper, treat the flare promptly,
 and/or add IMT — not a reason to delay or withhold local treatment
.
- Periocular steroid injection is contraindicated in necrotizing scleritis
 (risk of accelerating scleral thinning/perforation) —.
- A unilateral breakthrough of uveitic macular edema in a patient otherwise
 quiet bilaterally on systemic IMT should be treated first with regional
 therapy (sub-Tenon triamcinolone) — escalating or switching systemic IMT, or
 proceeding to vitrectomy, is disproportionate without evidence of active
 systemic/bilateral flare.


Early/upfront IMT (rather than waiting for steroid failure) is the
recommended strategy for diseases carrying a high risk of severe,
vision-threatening relapse: Behçet disease, sympathetic ophthalmia, and
chronic recurrent VKH.


Topical steroid pharmacology:
- Difluprednate 0.05% is MORE potent and penetrates tissue better than
 prednisolone acetate 1% — not less, a frequently reversed fact.
- "Soft" steroids — loteprednol and fluorometholone — are specifically
 formulated (via ester linkages rapidly metabolized to inactive compounds
 after their anti-inflammatory effect) for lower IOP-elevating potential,
 making them preferred for longer-term use or in known steroid responders
.


Antimetabolites and alkylating agents:
- Methotrexate is a folic-acid analogue/antifolate; folate (folinic
 acid/leucovorin) supplementation is routinely co-administered to reduce
 toxicity (GI upset, hepatotoxicity, myelosuppression) without
 significantly blunting its anti-inflammatory efficacy at uveitis doses
 (mechanisms beyond pure antifolate activity, e.g. adenosine release, are
 thought to underlie its benefit) — advising patients to avoid folate "to
 preserve effectiveness" reverses correct practice.
- Cyclosporine: nephrotoxicity, hypertension. Cyclophosphamide:
 myelosuppression, hemorrhagic cystitis.


Biologics:
- Anti-TNF agents (adalimumab, infliximab) are the most commonly used
 biologic class for uveitis — not anti-ICAM-1, which is not a mainstream
 uveitis biologic target at all.
- Adalimumab is FDA-approved for non-infectious uveitis and is
 increasingly considered first-line (not merely rescue) therapy for severe
 Behçet uveitis, particularly with posterior involvement.
- Infliximab IS effective for ocular inflammatory disease — "not
 effective" is a tested false statement — and its rapid onset of action
 suits acute, vision-threatening Behçet panuveitis flares; however, as a
 chimeric (part-murine) antibody it carries materially higher
 immunogenicity risk than the fully humanized adalimumab.
- Because infliximab is chimeric, it can provoke human anti-chimeric
 antibodies (HACA), reducing long-term efficacy and contributing to
 infusion reactions; concurrent low-dose methotrexate can reduce this risk
.
- Rituximab: chimeric monoclonal antibody against CD20+ B cells
.
- Interferon alfa-2a/2b: depression is a well-recognized, clinically
 important adverse effect.
- Biologic immunosuppression carries increased risk of malignancy and
 hepatitis B reactivation — screen accordingly before starting
.
- Adalimumab (and anti-TNF agents as a class) carries a recognized risk of
 inducing new or worsening pre-existing CNS demyelinating disease
 (e.g., multiple sclerosis); screen for personal/family MS history before
 starting anti-TNF therapy, and favor a non-TNF biologic (rituximab,
 tocilizumab) when that history is present. This is distinct from
 interferon-beta, an actual MS treatment — interferon-alfa, used in
 uveitis, carries no such MS benefit.


Drug-induced uveitis
Rifabutin — used mainly for prophylaxis/treatment of mycobacterial (e.g., Mycobacterium avium complex) infection, particularly in HIV/AIDS.
- Causes acute anterior uveitis (AAU), typically with hypopyon; associated vitritis can be severe enough to be mistaken for endophthalmitis.
- Risk is increased by concomitant drugs that inhibit rifabutin metabolism — classically clarithromycin and fluconazole — which raise effective rifabutin exposure.
- Treatment: dose reduction or withdrawal of the drug.


Cidofovir — used for cytomegalovirus (CMV) retinitis in AIDS.
- Repeated intravenous infusions can cause AAU with few cells but marked fibrinous exudate; vitritis is common, and hypopyon may occur with long-term administration.
- Treatment with topical steroids and mydriatics is usually successful, often without needing to stop therapy — unlike rifabutin, where dose modification/withdrawal is the primary strategy.


Bisphosphonates — a drug class that inhibits bone resorption (osteoporosis and other conditions).
- Activate a T-cell subgroup; thought to underlie the uveitis (usually anterior) and scleritis sometimes seen after administration.
- Typically occurs within 2 days of starting a bisphosphonate, or earlier after intravenous administration (e.g., IV zoledronic acid/pamidronate) — an acute-phase-reaction-like, cytokine-mediated pattern rather than direct toxicity.
- Bisphosphonates are not a recognized cause of cotton-wool spots — a useful negative/distractor point, since CWS reflect retinal microvascular ischemia (hypertension, diabetes, HIV, anaemia, collagen vascular disease), a different mechanism entirely from the T-cell-mediated inflammatory reaction bisphosphonates produce.


Sulfonamides — uveitis is a rare reported association. Sulfonamides (including acetazolamide) can also precipitate ciliary effusion with myopia and angle closure and are a well-documented cause of Stevens–Johnson syndrome.


Fluoroquinolones — systemic administration, particularly moxifloxacin, has been associated with acute extensive anterior segment pigment dispersion (see also bilateral acute iris transillumination (BAIT) and bilateral acute depigmentation of the iris (BADI)). It is unclear whether the mechanism is primary inflammation or phototoxicity from drug-induced photosensitization in predisposed individuals.


Tumour necrosis factor (TNF) inhibitors (e.g., etanercept, infliximab, adalimumab) — although these drugs are themselves used to treat ocular inflammation, they can paradoxically cause uveitis as an adverse effect; induction of sarcoidosis has also been reported. Etanercept in particular has a reputation in this exam bank as comparatively ineffective for (and even associated with) uveitis, unlike infliximab/adalimumab, which are generally more effective uveitis therapies.


Immune checkpoint inhibitors (e.g., ipilimumab, nivolumab, pembrolizumab) — increasingly recognized cause of immune-related uveitis as part of the broader spectrum of immune-related adverse events from checkpoint blockade.


Not associated: topical dorzolamide (Trusopt) and other topical carbonic anhydrase inhibitors have no meaningful uveitis association — useful as the "least likely" answer among drugs affecting the eye.


Drug-induced optic neuropathy
Ethambutol — anti-tuberculous agent, usually part of a multidrug regimen.
- Overall safety profile favourable, but acute or chronic optic neuritis can occur.
- Toxicity is dose- and duration-dependent: incidence up to 18% at >35 mg/kg/day, but <1% at the standard 15 mg/kg/day dose.
- Typically occurs 3–6 months after starting treatment, though reported after only a few days in some cases.
- Renal dysfunction raises risk, since ethambutol is renally excreted.
- Symptoms: may be absent, but typically painless bilateral blurring (central, sometimes paracentral/peripheral) with possible impairment of colour vision.
- Signs: minimal-to-severe VA reduction; normal or slightly swollen discs with splinter haemorrhages; normal or sluggish pupils. Red–green dyschromatopsia is the most common objective colour vision abnormality, though subtle blue–yellow defects (undetectable on Ishihara) may be an earlier finding. Loss of contrast sensitivity is an early sign.
- Visual field defects: central or peripheral.
- Prognosis: good after stopping the drug, though recovery can take time; a minority sustain permanent visual impairment with optic atrophy.
- Screening: baseline VA and Ishihara testing before starting ethambutol, with the patient advised to report any visual disturbance; repeat testing every 6 months on standard dose. Stop ethambutol immediately if toxicity develops, and consider stopping isoniazid too if used concurrently.
- Dose cap: to reduce toxic optic neuropathy risk, ethambutol should not exceed 15 mg/kg/day.
- On OCT, ganglion cell-inner plexiform layer (GC-IPL) thinning precedes RNFL thinning in toxic optic neuropathies generally — GC-IPL analysis can reveal structural damage before visual field changes appear on perimetry.


Isoniazid — may very rarely cause toxic optic neuropathy; risk is higher when combined with ethambutol.


Amiodarone — optic neuropathy, probably from demyelination, affects 1–2% of patients on long-term therapy; almost certainly not dose-related.
- Distinguishing from non-arteritic anterior ischaemic optic neuropathy (NAION) (which also affects patients with systemic vascular disease, and may be more common in patients on amiodarone) is clinically important, as it determines whether to discontinue the drug.
- Presentation: sudden or insidious, unilateral or bilateral visual impairment after a mean of 6–9 months of treatment; about one-third are asymptomatic.
- Signs: unilateral or bilateral optic disc swelling in a majority, which may persist for a few months after stopping the drug — resolution is slow, not rapid, a frequently tested point.
- Features favouring amiodarone toxicity over typical NAION: crowded disc appearance, subacute (not acute) onset, bilateral involvement, longer duration of disc swelling, and diffuse (not altitudinal) field loss.
- Investigation: cranial imaging if bilateral disc swelling is present; visual field defects vary in configuration/severity and may be reversible or permanent.
- OCT can detect peripapillary RNFL atrophy even before field defects develop.
- Prognosis: changes persist if treatment stops, but sometimes do not progress if the drug is continued — a genuine clinical dilemma weighed against the cardiac indication.


Vigabatrin — anti-epileptic (notably for infantile spasms/refractory epilepsy).
- Causes bilateral concentric, predominantly nasal visual field constriction in many long-term users, from photoreceptor and ganglion cell damage (not a classic optic neuritis).
- Uncommon at cumulative dose <1 kg, very common at >3 kg; maximum recommended adult daily dose is 3 g.
- Higher risk in males than females.
- Should be avoided in patients with pre-existing field defects and not combined with other retinotoxic drugs.
- Presentation: months to years after starting treatment; bilateral concentric or binasal field defects; may be asymptomatic, with normal VA unless scotomata approach fixation.
- Signs may be absent, but optic atrophy, peripheral atrophy, arteriolar narrowing, abnormal macular reflexes, and surface wrinkling can occur.
- The field loss is classically irreversible, and reported to affect up to roughly 30–50% of long-term users in some estimates — a key reason vigabatrin is reserved for refractory epilepsy rather than first-line use.


Methotrexate — a very rare cause of acute or gradual visual loss from optic neuropathy, with reduced VA, dyschromatopsia, and visual field loss; may be linked to folic acid metabolism (folate levels should be checked). Vitamin B12 and folate supplementation may help.


Chloroquine / hydroxychloroquine retinopathy
Indications and pharmacology. Chloroquine was historically used for malaria prophylaxis/treatment; resistance has reduced its use. Hydroxychloroquine is widely used for long-term inflammatory joint/skin disease (rheumatoid arthritis, SLE, and other connective tissue disease). Both concentrate in melanin-containing structures (RPE, choroid, and also the ciliary body/iris), explaining the ocular tropism of toxicity. HCQ is not stored in fat — meaning obesity can lead to dosing miscalculation if actual/total body weight rather than real/lean body weight is used.


Dosing and risk stratification.
- Chloroquine: risk rises significantly above a cumulative dose of 300 g; individual susceptibility is highly variable.
- Hydroxychloroquine is much safer than chloroquine, particularly when kept <5 mg/kg real body weight/day (current AAO 2016 guidance; this superseded the older 6.5 mg/kg ideal-body-weight rule, though the same fat-excluding dosing logic applies).
- Reported retinal toxicity risk: ~7.5% after >5 years of use; 20–50% of patients on HCQ for >20 years show some sign of retinopathy.
- Risk rises sharply above a cumulative dose of 1000 g, equivalent to a standard 200 mg twice-daily dose for ~7 years.
- Renal impairment and concurrent tamoxifen use increase risk (tamoxifen does not protect against it — a tested reversal). Obesity (via dosing miscalculation), advanced age, and pre-existing retinal/macular disease are additional risk factors. Renal (not hepatic) clearance is the relevant organ system — hepatic disease is not part of the standard risk-factor list.
- Vortex keratopathy may co-occur but, unlike retinopathy, keratopathy from these drugs is dose/duration-independent.


Diagnosis — the concept of premaculopathy. The goal of screening is to detect toxicity at the earliest, pre-visible stage ("premaculopathy" — functional/structural change before any fundus signs), since damage can still progress even after the drug is stopped once it has advanced beyond this stage.
- High-resolution OCT: relatively sensitive; shows parafoveal outer retinal thinning, loss of the cone outer-segment tip line, and disruption of the inner-segment/outer-segment junction as early features. The inferotemporal macula is typically the earliest site of hydroxychloroquine toxicity. Patients of Asian descent more often show a pericentral/extramacular pattern of damage rather than the classic parafoveal ring seen in many Caucasian patients — a screening-relevant ethnic difference.
- Multifocal ERG (mfERG): can demonstrate early paracentral response reduction when available.
- Visual fields: subtle central defects (Humphrey 10-2, Amsler grid) may be detected, but standard/conventional perimetry is less sensitive than the imaging modalities above. Early toxicity classically presents with bilateral paracentral field defects.
- Colour vision: mild defects may occur, but the commonly used Ishihara chart has relatively low sensitivity for this purpose.
- Fundus autofluorescence (FAF): shows paracentral ring hyper-/hypoautofluorescence in established toxicity, reflecting RPE change.


Clinical/imaging progression.
- Early maculopathy: modest VA reduction (6/9–6/12) with subtle macular disturbance; FAF and macular pigment density assessment are useful. With progressive perifoveal outer retinal loss but preserved central foveal tissue, OCT shows the characteristic "flying saucer" sign.
- Moderate–severe: VA drops to 6/36–6/60, with the classic "bull's-eye" macular lesion — a foveolar island of pigment surrounded by a depigmented ring of RPE atrophy, itself encircled by a hyperpigmented ring. More advanced disease shows widespread RPE atrophy around the fovea, attenuated retinal arterioles, and peripheral pigment clumping. Fluorescein angiography classically shows a "bull's-eye" hyperfluorescence pattern, though OCT has superseded FA for this purpose.


Screening protocol.
- Baseline assessment before or soon after starting treatment: functional parameters plus macular OCT, both to serve as a future comparison baseline and to exclude pre-existing maculopathy (a potential relative contraindication).
- Annual review (central visual fields + SD-OCT at minimum) should begin after a maximum of 5 years in patients without special risk factors. Earlier review is appropriate for children, chloroquine (rather than HCQ) users, or those with supplementary risk factors (age-related maculopathy, cataract, substantial renal impairment, tamoxifen use).
- Discontinuation should be discussed with the patient's rheumatologist if toxicity is suspected — balancing ocular risk against control of the underlying systemic disease.


Differential diagnosis of bull's-eye maculopathy/central RPE atrophy: AMD with geographic atrophy, Stargardt disease, and cone dystrophy are all genuine differentials sharing overlapping fundus appearances. Tay-Sachs disease (infantile lysosomal storage disease with a macular cherry-red spot) is an implausible differential given its entirely different age group, neurodegenerative course, and fundus appearance.


Phenothiazine, digoxin & other retinopathies
Phenothiazines — pigmentary retinopathy.
- Thioridazine — used for schizophrenia/psychoses; normal dose 150–600 mg/day. Doses >800 mg/day for just a few weeks can cause reduced VA and impaired dark adaptation. Progressive toxicity: (1) early "salt and pepper" pigmentary disturbance in the mid-periphery and posterior pole; (2) progression to plaque-like pigmentation with focal RPE/choriocapillaris loss; (3) eventually diffuse loss of RPE and choriocapillaris.
- Chlorpromazine — normal dose 75–300 mg/day; retinal toxicity is a risk only with substantially larger doses over prolonged periods, producing non-specific pigmentary granularity/clumping (milder than thioridazine). Chlorpromazine also causes vortex keratopathy and anterior lens capsule deposits.
- Both phenothiazines and hydroxychloroquine/chlorpromazine act directly on the RPE/photoreceptor complex — contrast with interferon alfa (below), which acts via a microvascular mechanism instead.


Digitalis (digoxin).
- Used for atrial fibrillation and heart failure; narrow therapeutic margin. Toxicity produces digestive, neurological, and visual disturbance: decreased VA, scotomas, photophobia, and dyschromatopsia — most commonly detected on formal colour testing.
- Classic colour disturbances: xanthopsia (yellow vision), also cyanopsia (blue) and chloropsia (green).
- ERG: delayed implicit time and reduced B-wave amplitude.
- Distractor point: colour vision defects are classically attributed to digoxin, NOT calcium channel blockers — a commonly confused pairing on exams.


Drug-induced crystalline maculopathies.
- Tamoxifen — anti-oestrogen for breast carcinoma; normal dose 20–40 mg/day. Ocular complications uncommon, and retinal toxicity mainly occurs with higher doses, rarely at standard doses. Retinopathy: bilateral fine yellow crystalline deposits in the inner retinal layers plus punctate grey lesions in the outer retina/RPE; visual impairment relates to maculopathy including foveolar cyst formation. OCT is a sensitive detection method. Rare side effect: optic neuritis, reversible on stopping the drug.
- Canthaxanthin — a carotenoid used (often in high doses) to simulate sun tanning. Prolonged use → innocuous glistening yellow inner retinal deposits in a doughnut/annular configuration at the posterior poles; slowly reversible.
- Methoxyflurane — inhalant general anaesthetic metabolized to oxalic acid, which combines with calcium to form insoluble salts deposited in tissues including RPE. Prolonged use can cause renal failure/secondary hyperoxalosis; ocular findings are calcium oxalate crystals scattered throughout the retina with mild visual impairment, followed by RPE hyperplasia at the posterior pole.
- Nitrofurantoin — antibiotic for UTIs; long-term use may cause slight visual impairment with superficial/deep glistening intraretinal deposits in a circinate pattern at the posterior pole.
- Non-drug causes of macular crystals (for differential purposes): primary hyperoxaluria, Bietti crystalline corneoretinal dystrophy, cystinosis, Sjögren–Larsson syndrome, gyrate atrophy, acquired parafoveal telangiectasia, talc–corn starch emboli, West African crystalline maculopathy.


Other drugs causing retinopathy (non-crystalline).
- Interferon alfa — used for hepatitis C and various malignancies. Retinopathy, particularly at high dose, is characterized by cotton-wool spots and retinal haemorrhages; FA shows focal areas of capillary non-perfusion — a microvascular mechanism, distinguishing it from drugs that directly damage the RPE/photoreceptor complex (hydroxychloroquine, chlorpromazine). Usually resolves spontaneously on stopping; visual prognosis generally good. Less common effects: cystoid macular oedema, extraocular muscle paresis, optic disc oedema, retinal vein occlusion.
- Desferrioxamine (deferoxamine) — chelating agent for chronic iron overload (e.g., transfusion-dependent conditions), usually given by slow subcutaneous infusion. Presents with rapid visual loss; fundus initially normal or mildly grey, with mottled pigmentary changes developing within weeks, plus abnormal electrodiagnostics. FA shows widespread punctate hyperfluorescence.
- Nicotinic acid — cholesterol-lowering agent; a minority of patients on >1.5 g/day develop a cystoid maculopathy (without FA leakage) causing mild VA reduction, resolving on stopping the drug.
- Alkyl nitrites ("poppers") — recreational drugs used before sexual activity; can rarely cause toxic maculopathy, typically in young patients with bilateral acute VA loss and photopsia. A small yellow foveal spot is seen clinically and on autofluorescence; OCT shows a selective defect in the foveal inner-segment ellipsoid band. No treatment; spontaneous recovery of visual function can occur.
- MEK inhibitors (targeted cancer therapy) — cause bilateral serous subretinal fluid from RPE pump/barrier dysfunction ("MEK inhibitor-associated retinopathy"), typically reversible — mechanistically distinct from chronic cytotoxic photoreceptor damage.


Drug-induced & toxic keratopathy
Vortex (whorl) keratopathy (cornea verticillata): a whorl-like pattern of fine epithelial/subepithelial deposits radiating from a point usually inferior to the visual axis, caused by lysosomal phospholipid accumulation in the basal epithelium. Classic causative drugs include amiodarone, chloroquine/hydroxychloroquine, indomethacin, tamoxifen, and chlorpromazine. Notably, ciprofloxacin (and fluoroquinolones generally) is the recognized exception among commonly-tested drug lists for vortex keratopathy — ciprofloxacin is well known for causing white corneal precipitates with topical use after corneal surgery/abrasion (a different, localized crystalline deposition phenomenon) but is not a classic systemic cause of whorl-pattern vortex keratopathy, making it the odd one out when a question asks "which of these does NOT cause vortex keratopathy." Vortex keratopathy itself is typically benign, reversible (or at least non-progressive), and asymptomatic, rarely requiring discontinuation of the causative systemic medication purely for ocular reasons — surgical intervention is essentially never indicated for vortex keratopathy alone.


Clinical evolution of vortex keratopathy (approximate chronological order as it develops): (1) a fine golden-brown horizontal line of opacities in the lower corneal epithelium, resembling the common age-related Hudson–Stähli iron line; (2) several irregular branching horizontal lines resembling cat whiskers; (3) with more branches, a full whorled pattern centred below the pupil, usually sparing the limbus; (4) associated pigmented clumps and iron deposition may follow.


Drug-specific detail:
- Amiodarone — virtually all patients on long-term therapy develop some keratopathy, often soon after starting; higher dose/longer duration → more substantial change. Vision is minimally impaired in only ~5% (loss of a single Snellen line, mild blur, haloes) — rarely enough to justify stopping the drug. Reverses slowly on discontinuation. Amiodarone also causes anterior subcapsular lens deposits and optic neuropathy.
- Chloroquine/hydroxychloroquine — unlike chloroquine retinopathy, the vortex keratopathy they cause bears no relationship to dosage or duration; it is usually reversible on stopping therapy and sometimes clears even with continued use.
- Others: numerous additional drugs occasionally cause vortex keratopathy; Fabry disease is a non-drug phenocopy.
- Chlorpromazine — subtle diffuse yellowish-brown granular deposits in the corneal endothelium, Descemet membrane, and deep stroma, confined to the interpalpebral fissure area, on long-term therapy. Also causes anterior lens capsule deposits and (rarely) retinopathy.
- Argyrosis — discoloration from silver deposition (iatrogenic or occupational), producing greyish-brown granular deposits in Descemet membrane; conjunctiva may also be affected. Note the naming trap: "argyrosis/argyria" = silver, from Greek argyros; do not confuse with chrysiasis.
- Chrysiasis — deposition of gold in tissue after prolonged chrysotherapy (gold salts for rheumatoid arthritis); named from Greek chrysos (gold). Virtually all patients receiving a cumulative dose >1500 mg develop dust-like/glittering purple-granule corneal deposits concentrated in the deep stroma and periphery. Innocuous and not an indication to stop treatment; deposits may clear after stopping or may persist. Gold can also cause innocuous lens deposits and occasionally marginal keratitis.
- Amantadine (oral, for Parkinson disease) — diffuse white punctate epithelial opacities ± epithelial oedema, appearing 1–2 weeks after starting the drug, resolving with discontinuation.


Benzalkonium chloride (BAK) toxicity: chronic exposure to BAK-preserved topical medications (classically multi-drug glaucoma regimens used over years) damages the ocular surface — causing superficial punctate keratopathy, conjunctival hyperemia, reduced tear break-up time, and sometimes subconjunctival fibrosis that can complicate future filtration surgery. In a glaucoma patient on multiple long-term preserved drops who develops keratopathy, the appropriate management pivot is to switch to preservative-free formulations of the same medications rather than simply adding more lubrication or changing drug class for ocular-surface reasons alone.


Topical anesthetic abuse keratopathy: chronic, often surreptitious, self-administration of topical anesthetic drops (proparacaine, tetracaine — sometimes obtained inappropriately, or used to mask ongoing pain from another condition) causes progressive corneal epithelial breakdown, stromal infiltration (which can mimic infectious keratitis), and in severe chronic cases, a ring-shaped stromal infiltrate and significant thinning. Corneal ectasia is not a recognized feature of this entity — describing anesthetic abuse keratopathy as causing progressive ectasia (rather than the actual pattern of epithelial defect, ring infiltrate, and risk of melt/perforation from a flat, non-ectatic process) is a false-statement distractor. Management is identifying and stopping the anesthetic abuse (often requiring a high index of suspicion, since patients may conceal the behavior), supportive care, and treating any secondary infection.


Cataract morphology & drug associations
Nuclear cataract — progressive yellow-to-brown ("brunescent") discolouration and sclerosis of the central lens nucleus from age-related protein cross-linking/pigment accumulation.
- Causes an index myopia shift (increased nuclear refractive index) — disproportionately impairs distance vision while near vision may even transiently improve.
- "Second sight of the elderly" — the myopic shift can transiently neutralize presbyopia/age-related hyperopia, letting a previously presbyopic patient read without glasses again.
- Dim/night vision is disproportionately affected: pupil dilation in low light lengthens the optical path through the cloudy nucleus and removes the pinhole benefit miosis gives in bright light.
- Advanced brunescent cataracts impair blue-yellow colour discrimination (short-wavelength light is preferentially absorbed by the pigmented nucleus).
- "Oil droplet cataract" is not a nuclear-sclerosis sign — it is a distinct glistening, spherical, fetal-nucleus opacity pathognomonic of galactosaemia (see Congenital cataract note).


Cortical cataract — wedge-shaped (spoke-like) peripheral cortical opacities progressing centripetally; classically produces monocular diplopia/ghosting from irregular light scatter through patchy opacities, and disproportionate glare.


Posterior subcapsular cataract (PSC) — opacity at the posterior cortex just anterior to the capsule.
- The fastest-growing morphological type and causes the most glare/disability for a given lens clarity — its central, posterior, visual-axis location means even a small PSC disproportionately scatters/blocks light, especially with miosis (reading, bright light).
- Disproportionately impairs near vision (reading) relative to distance, the opposite pattern from nuclear cataract.
- Tends to affect younger patients than nuclear/cortical cataract.
- Histology: swollen, aberrant lens epithelial cells migrating posteriorly — "Wedl (bladder) cells."
- Glare testing in PSC must be performed undilated, since dilation bypasses the central opacity and underestimates functional impairment.
- Risk factors beyond ageing: ocular trauma, intraocular/periocular/systemic corticosteroids, intraocular inflammation (especially Fuchs heterochromic iridocyclitis — classically produces PSC, not nuclear, cataract), ionizing radiation, diabetes, and chronic alcohol use.


Drug- and toxin-induced lens change:
- Corticosteroids (any route — topical, periocular, systemic, even subconjunctival depot) → dose- and duration-dependent PSC, histologically indistinguishable from age-related PSC. Initially posterior subcapsular, with later anterior subcapsular involvement also possible. Individual susceptibility varies — children may be more susceptible than adults. The dose/duration-to-risk relationship is not fully linear, but higher doses and longer treatment carry greater risk; early opacities may regress if steroids are stopped promptly, though progression can still occur despite withdrawal. Once formed, steroid cataract is generally not reversible on stopping the drug (contrast with steroid-induced ocular hypertension, which typically IS reversible on cessation) — a frequently tested asymmetry.
- Miotics (echothiophate) and amiodarone and chlorpromazine/phenothiazines → anterior subcapsular opacities/pigment deposits (not posterior) — amiodarone produces whorl-like deposits paralleling its vortex/whorl keratopathy. Chlorpromazine specifically causes fine, stellate, yellowish-brown granules on the anterior lens capsule within the pupillary area in about 50% of patients who have received a cumulative dose of 1000 g; the deposits persist despite stopping the drug but are classically visually insignificant despite their conspicuous appearance.
- Gold (chrysotherapy) causes innocuous anterior capsular deposits in about 50% of patients treated for longer than 3 years — paralleling gold's corneal chrysiasis.
- Allopurinol (used for gout) increases cataract risk in elderly patients with a high cumulative dose or extended treatment duration.
- Statins: not established as an independent cataract risk in human studies on their own — but the combination of simvastatin + erythromycin (a CYP3A4 inhibitor interaction raising effective statin exposure) has been linked to increased cataract risk.
- Tamoxifen: possible PSC/retinopathy association reported in the literature; also a recognized cause of crystalline maculopathy.
- Smoking: causes oxidative lens damage; damage already present is generally partially reversible on cessation.
- Steroid-induced PSC in children: classic teaching holds it generally does not reverse once formed, though very early/mild cases caught promptly are sometimes described as partially regressing — a softer distinction than the steroid IOP-reversibility contrast above.
- Tamsulosin and other alpha-1 antagonists are not cataractogenic — their recognized ocular association is intraoperative floppy iris syndrome (IFIS) during cataract surgery, not accelerated lens opacification, a frequently tested distractor pairing.


Toxic/metabolic associations (covered in depth in their own notes where applicable):
- Chalcosis (intraocular copper, e.g., retained IOFB) → "sunflower cataract" — a distinct copper-coloured anterior capsular/subcapsular deposit, and a copper Descemet ring that must be distinguished from the true Kayser-Fleischer ring of Wilson disease.
- Myotonic dystrophy → polychromatic, multicoloured "Christmas tree" crystals in the lens — occurs alongside muscle weakness, ptosis, frontal balding, cardiac conduction defects, and testicular atrophy. (Myotonic dystrophy is not associated with ectopia lentis — a frequently tested distractor pairing.)
- Galactosaemia → bilateral "oil droplet" cataract, reversible if caught early (see Congenital cataract note).
- Diabetes → in young, poorly controlled patients, a distinctive bilateral "snowflake" PSC cataract via the polyol/sorbitol pathway (osmotic lens fibre swelling); older diabetics more typically show accelerated ordinary age-related nuclear/cortical change.


Drug-induced eyelid reactions
Epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) inhibitors (e.g., cetuximab, erlotinib, panitumumab) — used for many solid tumours. Effects are dose-dependent:
- Trichomegaly (abnormal eyelash overgrowth) is the most common finding.
- Blepharitis and dry eye syndrome are less common.
- Cicatricial ectropion is rare.
- These reactions reflect EGFR's role in normal follicular/epidermal turnover of periocular skin and adnexa; they are not an indication to stop an otherwise effective oncologic therapy, though symptomatic management (lash trimming, lubrication, lid hygiene) is appropriate.


Imatinib — a selective tyrosine kinase inhibitor used for chronic myeloid leukaemia (CML) and gastrointestinal stromal tumours (GIST).
- Up to two-thirds of patients develop periorbital oedema, typically 5–8 weeks after starting treatment.
- This is a mild, self-limited problem and does not require discontinuation of the drug — an important distractor point, since the high reported incidence might otherwise suggest a more serious reaction.


Ipilimumab — a CTLA-4 checkpoint inhibitor used for metastatic malignant melanoma.
- Can produce a syndrome of periorbital/eyelid symptoms and signs resembling thyroid eye disease (periorbital swelling, injection).
- This usually responds to systemic corticosteroids, consistent with its being an immune-related adverse event of checkpoint blockade.


CNV classification & anti-VEGF
Anatomic classification (the organizing framework):
- Type 1 (occult) MNV: neovascular tissue grows in the sub-RPE space, beneath the RPE and above Bruch's membrane. Because it sits directly under the RPE, it classically elevates the RPE off Bruch's membrane, producing a fibrovascular PED — making PED a typical OCT finding for this subtype. Non-exudative ("quiescent") neovascular AMD, which can remain non-exudative for a relatively long period, is more characteristic of Type 1 than Type 2. On FA, Type 1/occult lesions typically show irregular stippled hyperfluorescence (a fibrovascular PED pattern) or late leakage of undetermined source — NOT an early, bright, lacy, sharply-demarcated lesion (that description belongs to Type 2/classic).
- Type 2 (classic) MNV: neovascular tissue extends from the choroid, through a break in Bruch's membrane, into the subretinal space — between the RPE and neurosensory retina. Because the lesion is already above/anterior to the RPE rather than beneath it, Type 2 CNV does not characteristically elevate the RPE off Bruch's membrane, making PED a much less typical OCT finding for this subtype compared with Type 1, Type 3, or PCV. On FA, classic CNV's signature is an early, bright, lacy, sharply-demarcated hyperfluorescent lesion that begins leaking in later frames.
- Type 3 (retinal angiomatous proliferation, RAP): neovascular tissue originates from the retina's own deep capillary plexus and grows downward toward the RPE/choroid, forming a retinal-choroidal anastomosis — for staging detail. Mistaking this retinal-origin pattern for "Type 2" is a frequently tested error: Type 2 is choroidal in origin, not retinal. A PED can be seen with Type 3 lesions at the site of the retinal-choroidal anastomosis.
- PCV is generally considered a variant of Type 1/occult MNV (not Type 2/classic), sharing the sub-RPE location and frequently producing a PED (often with notched or haemorrhagic morphology); for full detail.
- OCT-based inference of subtype (a useful clue even without angiography): a fibrovascular PED with a relatively flat overlying neurosensory retina suggests Type 1/PCV; a subretinal hyperreflective lesion without significant RPE elevation suggests Type 2/classic CNV.


Anti-VEGF therapy:
- Agents in clinical use include ranibizumab, aflibercept, bevacizumab (off-label), brolucizumab, and the earlier, now largely historical, pegaptanib.
- Landmark trials establishing efficacy and dosing strategy include MARINA and ANCHOR (ranibizumab vs. sham/PDT, establishing anti-VEGF superiority), PIER and PrONTO (quarterly/as-needed dosing strategies), HORIZON and SAILOR (long-term safety/extension data), HARBOUR (ranibizumab dosing comparison), CATT and IVAN (head-to-head ranibizumab vs. bevacizumab, establishing comparable efficacy), and VIEW 1/VIEW 2 (aflibercept registration trials).
- PDT (photodynamic therapy with verteporfin) remains an option, particularly as adjunctive therapy for PCV — for its photochemical mechanism.
- Management of haemorrhagic AMD (large submacular/sub-RPE haemorrhage) may require adjunctive measures beyond anti-VEGF monotherapy, such as pneumatic displacement (intravitreal gas ± tPA) or vitrectomy with subretinal tPA/blood evacuation, given the poor natural prognosis of persistent submacular blood.
- Intravitreal injection technique follows a standard safety protocol: topical/povidone-iodine antisepsis, sterile lid speculum, measured distance from the limbus (typically 3.5mm phakic / 3.0mm pseudophakic), and post-injection IOP/light-perception check.


Bevacizumab cortical blindness
Drug and indication. Bevacizumab is used systemically for colon and rectal tumours (among other malignancies); this is distinct from its (much more common in ophthalmic practice) off-label intravitreal use for retinal/choroidal neovascular disease.


Clinical feature. May rarely result in cortical blindness, which is not fully reversible on stopping the medication — an important distinction from many of the other drug-toxicity syndromes in this chapter (e.g., ethambutol optic neuropathy, topiramate angle closure), which are more consistently reversible with prompt cessation.


Prognosis. Variable: cessation of the drug usually improves vision, but ~20% of patients may deteriorate further; final VA worse than 6/60 occurs in around 20% of eyes.


Screening. Some authors have suggested screening, but no consensus exists as to its benefit. Patients should be warned of the risk and told to report visual symptoms immediately.


Mechanism context. As an anti-VEGF agent, systemic bevacizumab's posterior cortical toxicity is thought to relate to disruption of VEGF-dependent vascular/neuronal support in the visual cortex — consistent with reports of reversible posterior leukoencephalopathy (PRES)-like syndromes with other anti-angiogenic/chemotherapeutic agents, though Kanski's text does not elaborate further on mechanism.
